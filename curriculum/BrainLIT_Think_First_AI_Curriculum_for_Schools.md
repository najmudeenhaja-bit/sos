# BrainLIT Think-First AI Curriculum for Schools

Oct 6, 2026 · Haja Najmudeen

A three-tier, project-based AI curriculum for Tamil Nadu students aged 8 to 12. In every activity, children solve a real local problem and take home something they made, with the thinking done first and AI used second.

Live, editable version: https://claude.ai/code/artifact/8b695ce3-500f-4b23-8a6c-6fe38be6e117

## At a glance

Each tier is one school year: 30 periods of 40 minutes, one period a week, June to March. A tier holds 10 projects. Each project runs over 3 periods (Think, Build, Show) and ends with a physical or digital output the child keeps. The year closes with a Demo Day for parents.

| Tier | Rank earned | Default grades (age) | AI ideas covered | How children use AI | What every child takes home |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. AI Literacy | Spark | Grades 3–4 (8–9) | What AI is and isn't, learning from examples, data and bias, wrong answers, privacy, prompting | Mostly unplugged; teacher drives AI on one projector; class trains a picture model together | 10-page printed portfolio, a picture book for KG children, a waste-sorting station, a family fact-check card |
| 2. Medium | Flame | Grades 5–6 (10–11) | Training and testing models, accuracy, sound and image recognition, the RACE prompt method, bias, simple chatbots | Small groups, one device per 4–5 children, no-login tools (Teachable Machine, Scratch + ML for Kids) | A working classifier, a cooker-whistle counter, a school help bot, a water audit report, a hazard map sent to the local councillor |
| 3. Advanced | Blaze | Grades 6–7 (11–12) | Data analysis, predictions, evaluating a model with real users, app building, AI ethics and policy | Pairs on lab devices with school-managed accounts; full Think → Build → Test → Improve loop | A working app, a tested crop-leaf model, a heritage audio guide, a policy paper to the principal, a capstone prototype and pitch |

Tiers are set by readiness, not only age. A Grade 6 class that has never done Tier 1 starts with a 6-period Tier 1 “fast track” before moving to Tier 2.

## Why this design

Competitors already do AI projects, gamification and AI ethics well. What none of them does is make **thinking before AI** the habit behind every project, with **Tamil Nadu problems** and **school-safe delivery**. That is the gap this curriculum fills.

### What competitors teach us

| Competitor | What they do well | Where they fall short for TN schools | What we do instead |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Code.org / CodeAI | Free AI literacy for schools, including ethics, bias and privacy | Depends on the teacher to make children practise; translated, not Tamil-context | Every project is a local problem; teacher scripts in Tanglish; ethics is practised in each project, not taught as a separate lesson |
| Tynker | Gamified, self-paced AI and coding projects | Self-paced, so children drop off; coding-first | Teacher-led and group-based; we reward thinking moves, not just finished levels |
| Create & Learn, BrightCHAMPS, Codingal | Live small-group classes, projects, parent visibility | AI is one subject among many; sold as paid packs to individual families; English-first | Built for the whole class during the school timetable |
| Codevidhya | School partnerships and teacher training | A coding syllabus, not problem-solving | Projects start from a problem the child can see, not from a tool or a syntax |
| Rewire Kids (Chennai) | Ships a website, an app and an image portfolio in 9 weekends | Grades 6–12 only; adult tools (Claude, Lovable, Bolt) need accounts children under 13 can't hold; tool-first | Ages 8–12; no-login or school-managed tools; problem-first, tool-second |

### Chitti, the closest competitor for this school offer

Chitti is a Chennai company founded by Premanand Sethurajan of LMES, whose Tamil YouTube channel has about 1.6 million subscribers. It sells to both parents and schools in Tamil Nadu, so the schools we pitch may already know it.

| | Chitti (from its public pages) | BrainLIT school curriculum |

| Modules | Aeromodelling, electronics, game development, toy engineering, rocket science, simple machines and junior robotics; later app development, Python, embedded systems and satellites | AI only: how AI learns, how to check it, how to use it on purpose, and how to build with it |
| How children learn | Build, test, fail and try again with physical kits | Think without AI, build with AI, then explain without notes |
| What the child makes | Kit models and coding projects | A solution to a real local problem, handed to a real person |
| What the school needs | A STEM lab and kits | A projector for Tier 1, group devices for Tier 2, and a lab for Tier 3 |
| Tamil reach | Strong: the LMES audience and Tamil video lessons | Tanglish teacher scripts; no audience yet |

**Inference from the module lists:** for Chitti, AI is one track among many STEM tracks. Questioning and checking AI output is not a visible focus. **Our pitch line:** “Chitti builds young makers; BrainLIT builds AI thinkers.” A school with a Chitti lab can run BrainLIT alongside it.

**What we borrow from Chitti:**

- **Hands-on builds keep children hooked.** At least 3 projects per tier include a physical build from cardboard, paper or scrap, costing under Rs 50 per group, so no paid kits are needed.
- **Failure as information.** Every Think Book has a Fail Log: what didn't work, why, and what I changed.
- **Teacher certification as a product.** Teachers who complete training and teach a full tier become “BrainLIT Certified Think-First Teachers.”
- **Tamil-first trust.** Each project has a 2-minute Tamil explainer video that parents receive on WhatsApp.

### What our after-school cohort taught us (BrainLIT Cohort 1, May 2026)

- **Printed outputs travel home.** The Lie Detector Report went on fridges, and the storybook got the strongest parent reaction. Every school project here ends in something you can hold, print or demo.
- **The “catch AI lying” moment works.** The invented-school demo made children feel the problem with trusting AI. Each tier keeps one such moment.
- **Named muscles and ranks keep children coming back.** Children who knew they were working toward Spark stayed engaged. We map Spark, Flame and Blaze onto the three tiers.
- **Speaking without notes builds confidence.** The 60-second stand and the debate video were the strongest proof of thinking, so every project ends with a short Show.
- **Real person, real use.** In Session 6 (“build for a named person”), children cared most about the work. Each project names who it is for.
- **What needs to change for schools:** class sizes of 35–45, not 15. Children under 13 can't hold their own ChatGPT accounts. Many schools have only a projector. Class teachers, not founders, will run the lessons, so they need full scripts.

### Parent worries we answer

| Parent worry (from our research) | How the curriculum answers it |
| --- | --- |
| “AI will make my child stop thinking” | Every project opens with a no-AI Think step. AI is allowed only after the child has written their own idea. |
| “Is my child really preparing for the AI world?” | A clear 3-year path with named skills and ranks |
| “My child starts but doesn't continue” | 3-period projects with a visible output, and ranks earned for thinking |
| “Impressive output, but does my child understand it?” | Every Show step has the child explain choices aloud without notes |
| Data safety and photo misuse | No student accounts on public chatbots, no student face photos uploaded, and parental consent under the DPDP Act |
| Not every family has a laptop | All work happens in school; Tier 1 needs only a projector |

## The Think-First method

Every project follows the same three-period rhythm, so teachers learn it once and children build a habit: **think first, then use AI, then explain.**

**Period 1 — THINK (no AI).** *Listen and Interrogate.*

1. **Meet the problem.** Use a real photo, story or visit from the school, the street or home. Name who is affected.
2. **Ask before answering.** Each child writes 3 questions and 1 idea of their own in the Think Book before anyone touches AI.
3. **Gather local facts.** Run a mini survey, count or interview. This is the context AI doesn't have.

**Period 2 — BUILD (AI as a tool).** *Use AI on purpose.*

4. **Direct the AI** with RACE (Role, Action, Context, Example) in Tiers 2–3, or “Who, What, For whom” in Tier 1.
5. **Check the AI.** Ask the 5 questions: Says who? Compared to what? What's missing? Who benefits? What if I'm wrong?
6. **Create** the output and mark clearly which parts are the child's and which are AI's.

**Period 3 — SHOW (Think Aloud).** *Explain and deliver.*

7. **Improve** using feedback from a classmate or the real user.
8. **Explain in 60 seconds, without notes:** “I chose \_\_\_ because \_\_\_. AI got \_\_\_ wrong.”
9. **Deliver** the output to the real person it was made for (a KG class, the canteen, a grandparent, the principal) and add it to the portfolio.

### The 5 Thinking Muscles (what we actually assess)

| Muscle | What it is | Tanglish cue the teacher uses |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Q — Question | Asking a better question than the obvious one | “Adhe kelvi sari-yaa irukka?” |
| S — Skeptic | Checking before believing, AI included | “Nambaadhey — kelu.” |
| C — Context | Spotting what's missing; bringing local knowledge | “Enna vishayam theriyala?” |
| E — Empathy | Asking whose view is missing; building for a real person | “Yaaru paarthu illai?” |
| Co — Courage | Saying and defending what you actually think | “Nee nijama enna nineikkirey?” |

**Gamified thinking, not just finished levels.** Children earn class “Sparks” (stickers or points) for thinking moves: a sharp question, catching an AI mistake, or giving a clear reason. Completing a tier earns its rank: Spark, then Flame, then Blaze.

## Tier 1 — AI Literacy (Spark rank, Grades 3–4, ages 8–9)

By the end of Tier 1, a child can explain what AI is, show that it learns from examples, catch it being wrong, and keep their own data safe. Children never log in to an AI tool. The teacher runs AI on one projector, and the children do the thinking on paper.

**Minimum setup:** one teacher laptop with a projector and internet. Teachable Machine and Quick, Draw! need no login.

| # | Project | Real problem (who it helps) | AI idea | How AI is used | Tangible output | Muscles |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | AI vs Me | Children think AI is smarter than them at everything | AI processes words; humans live experiences | Teacher asks AI “What does a Chennai bus stand smell like?” and children list what's missing | AI Identity Card (A5, printed): “AI can… / Only I can… because…” | Q, C |
| 2 | AI Around My House | Paati and Thatha are confused or afraid of “AI” on their phones | AI vs ordinary machines; rules vs learning | Unplugged card sort (fan, calculator, YouTube suggestions, Google Maps), then teacher checks with AI | Bilingual “AI in our house” guide card for a grandparent, signed by the child | C, E |
| 3 | Teach the Machine: Sort Our Waste | Classroom bins are mixed, and cleaning staff sort them by hand | Machines learn from examples (training data) | Class photographs wet, dry and recyclable items; teacher trains Teachable Machine live, and children predict, then test | Labelled 3-bin sorting station for the classroom + a class-trained model demo | Q, E |
| 4 | Does AI Know Tamil Nadu? | AI drawing games don't recognise kolam, idli or an auto-rickshaw | Data gaps cause bias | Children draw local objects in Quick, Draw! and record which ones AI misses | “Teach AI Tamil Nadu” picture book of 50 class drawings + a tally chart of AI hits and misses | C, E |
| 5 | Fact Detective Jr | Fake cures and fake holiday news in family WhatsApp groups | AI and the internet can be confidently wrong | Teacher shows AI inventing a fake “famous school”; teams check 5 claims in books and on trusted sites | “Stop – Check – Share” fridge card for home + a 3-claim Lie Detector Report | S |
| 6 | Ask Better, Get Better | The school needs a monsoon-safety message for juniors | Prompts: who, what, for whom | Children write prompts on paper; teacher runs a weak, a better and a best version side by side | Monsoon Safety poster for the notice board (Tamil + English) | Q |
| 7 | My Story, AI's Pictures | The KG class has few Tamil picture books about their own lives | AI images follow your words; AI can't see your memories | Child writes a 4-scene story from their life; teacher generates pictures from the child's descriptions; class compares imagined vs generated | 6-page printed bilingual picture book, donated to the KG library | C, E |
| 8 | Robot Helper | A grandparent forgets medicine times | Algorithms: step-by-step instructions, if-then | Unplugged: “program” a classmate-robot; then check the steps with AI | Illustrated medicine-time chart for a real grandparent + an if-then instruction card | E, Q |
| 9 | My Data Shield | Children share photos, addresses and school names in games and comments | AI learns from data; photos can be changed (morphing) | Teacher shows a harmless edited photo of a cartoon; class lists what is safe to share | “My Data Shield” pledge card + a class privacy charter poster | S, Co |
| 10 | Spark Fair | Parents can't see what their child has learned | Explaining your thinking | Gallery walk; each child presents one project in 60 seconds, with no notes | 10-page printed portfolio + Spark certificate | Co |

**Signature moment:** the fake-school hallucination demo in Project 5, which is the “feel it” moment our cohort remembered most.

## Tier 2 — Medium (Flame rank, Grades 5–6, ages 10–11)

By the end of Tier 2, children in groups can train and test their own models, measure how often they are right, direct AI with RACE, and build something a real person uses. They work hands-on in groups of 4–5 using no-login tools. The teacher still runs any chatbot or image generator on the projector.

**Minimum setup:** 1 laptop or tablet per group, with a webcam and microphone. Tools: Teachable Machine, Scratch with Machine Learning for Kids, Canva for Education (school account).

| # | Project | Real problem (who it helps) | AI idea | How AI is used | Tangible output | Muscles |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Smart Sorting Bin | The canteen's waste goes to landfill unsorted | Training vs testing data; accuracy | Groups train an image model on canteen waste, then test it on 20 new items and log right and wrong | Cardboard smart bin with the working classifier on a tablet, an accuracy chart, and bin labels fixed in the canteen | Q, S |
| 2 | Cooker Whistle Counter | Amma or Paati loses count of pressure-cooker whistles | Sound recognition | Groups record whistles vs kitchen noise, train an audio model, and build a Scratch counter that announces “3 whistle aachu!” | Working whistle counter + a demo video sent home | E, C |
| 3 | Water Watch | School taps leak while the city faces summer shortages | Data shows patterns; AI can summarise but needs checking | Measure each tap's leak with a cup and a stopwatch; teacher asks AI to suggest fixes; children check each suggestion | Water Audit Report + an infographic presented to the principal | C, S |
| 4 | Study Buddy | Juniors struggle with a hard lesson (for example, the water cycle) | The RACE prompt method | Groups write RACE prompts; teacher runs them; children fact-check every line against the textbook | Revision card set + a 10-question quiz, given to a Grade 3 class | Q |
| 5 | Forward Fighters | Fake AI images and videos spread in parents' WhatsApp groups | Deepfakes and AI images; checking the source | “Real or AI?” image game; build a checklist; test it on 10 viral images | 60-second Tanglish voice note or video for the parents' group + a printed checklist | S, Co |
| 6 | Bias Busters | Stereotypes about who can be a doctor, farmer or engineer | Bias in training data | Teacher asks an image AI to “draw a doctor, a farmer, an engineer”; class tallies who appears, then writes fair prompts | Bias Report poster with before-and-after images, shown in the corridor | E, S |
| 7 | School Help Bot | New parents keep asking the office the same questions (bus timings, fees, uniform) | Chatbots: intents and text classification | Groups collect 30 real questions, train a text model in ML for Kids, and build a Scratch bot that answers | Working bilingual FAQ bot on the reception laptop | E, Q |
| 8 | Safe Route to School | Open drains, traffic and stray dogs on the walk to school | Grouping (categorising) data | Survey 50 schoolmates; teacher asks AI to group complaints; children check that the groups are right | Hazard map + a signed letter to the ward councillor or traffic police | C, Co |
| 9 | Heat Wave Helper | Heat illness in April and May, for children and elders | AI advice vs official advice | AI drafts heat tips; children check each tip against official health-department guidance and correct it | Classroom heat-safety kit: hydration chart, alert poster, and a Tamil audio announcement for assembly | S, E |
| 10 | Flame Demo Day | The school wants proof the children can solve problems | Combining all of the above | Each group picks a problem from its own street and runs the full loop | 2-page Problem–Solution Brief (problem, surprising finding, what AI missed, our proposal) + a 2-minute group pitch + Flame certificate | All 5 |

**Signature moment:** the Cooker Whistle Counter in Project 2. It works at home the same evening, so parents see it working.

## Tier 3 — Advanced (Blaze rank, Grades 6–7, ages 11–12)

By the end of Tier 3, a child can collect and analyse real data, build and test a working app or model with real users, judge whether an AI system is fair, and defend a decision in public. Children work in pairs in the computer lab. Only school-managed accounts are used, with parental consent.

**Minimum setup:** computer lab with 1 device per 2 children; Google Workspace for Education or similar school accounts. Tools: Teachable Machine, MIT App Inventor, Google Sheets, Scratch, plus the teacher's AI assistant on the projector.

| # | Project | Real problem (who it helps) | AI idea | How AI is used | Tangible output | Muscles |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Canteen Food Waste Detective | Kilos of lunch go into the bin every day | Data collection, patterns, before-and-after testing | Weigh waste for 10 days in Sheets; AI suggests patterns; pairs check each one against the data | Data report + a menu-change pitch to the canteen, re-measured 2 weeks later | C, S |
| 2 | Flood-Ready Street | Streets flood every northeast monsoon | Predictions from past data, and their limits | Map past waterlogging from family memories and rainfall records; AI drafts a plan; pairs fix what it got wrong for their street | Family flood-readiness plan + a street map shared with the residents' association | C, E |
| 3 | Plant Doctor | The school garden and farming families lose plants to leaf disease | Model evaluation: accuracy, false alarms, test with real users | Train an image model on healthy vs diseased leaves, then test it with a gardener or farmer and record results | Tested model + accuracy report + a care card in Tamil | S, E |
| 4 | Medicine Reminder App | Grandparents miss medicine doses | Apps that use AI features (Tamil text-to-speech) | Interview the grandparent, sketch screens on paper first, then build in MIT App Inventor | Working Android app installed on a family phone | E, Q |
| 5 | Does AI Hear Us? | Voice assistants misunderstand Tamil and Tamil-accented English | Fairness testing of an AI system | Record 20 sentences from 5 family members; count speech-to-text errors by speaker | Fairness Report with an error chart + 3 recommendations | S, E |
| 6 | Tamil Word Quest | Juniors find Tamil vocabulary hard, and AI translation makes Tamil mistakes | Machine translation and its limits | Collect AI translation errors; pairs build a Scratch quiz game with correct answers | Bilingual quiz game played by Grade 3 + an “AI Tamil Mistakes” log | C, S |
| 7 | Heritage Audio Guide | Children don't know the history of their local temple, fort or market | AI research vs primary sources | AI drafts a history; pairs check it against interviews with elders and local records, and cut what is unproven | QR-code board with child-voiced audio clips, placed at the site or in school | C, E |
| 8 | AI Court | Should our school use face-recognition attendance? | Ethics, privacy, consent and trade-offs | Teams argue for and against, then debate the AI's best argument with no notes | 90-second debate videos + a policy recommendation letter to the principal | Co, E |
| 9–10 | Capstone: Solve for My Street | Teams choose a real problem in their own area | The full loop: Think, Question, Research, AI, Create, Improve, Showcase | 6 periods: problem brief, build, test with 3 real users, improve, pitch | Working prototype (app, model, campaign or physical build) + pitch deck + a 3-minute pitch at Blaze Demo Day + Blaze certificate | All 5 |

**Capstone showcase:** the best capstones from partner schools go to an inter-school BrainLIT Junior Innovators day. Parents, local officials and the press are invited.

## Running it in a school

Schools pick one of three timetable models. All three use the same projects, teacher guides and portfolio.

| Model | Time per tier | Who teaches | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| In the timetable | 30 periods × 40 min (1 a week, June–March) | Class or computer teacher, trained by BrainLIT | Schools that want AI for every child in the grade |
| After-school AI Club | 15 weeks × 2 sessions of 60 min | BrainLIT facilitator, or a trained school teacher | Schools starting small, with interested children only |
| Summer or holiday bootcamp | 10 days × 2 hours (5 core projects + Demo Day) | BrainLIT facilitator | A pilot before signing for a full year |

### Device modes (so no school is excluded)

- **Mode A — One screen:** teacher laptop + projector. Enough for all of Tier 1 and for the AI-demo parts of Tiers 2–3.
- **Mode B — Group devices:** 1 device per 4–5 children. Needed for Tier 2.
- **Mode C — Lab:** 1 device per 2 children with school accounts. Needed for Tier 3.
- Each project's teacher guide has a paper-only fallback for days when the internet or power goes down.

### Child-safety rules (non-negotiable)

1. Children under 13 never create accounts on public chatbots. AI chat and image tools run only on the teacher's account, shown on the projector.
2. No photos of children's faces are uploaded to any AI tool. Training photos show objects, leaves, waste and places only.
3. Written parental consent is collected before any school account is used or any child's voice or video is recorded, in line with India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023.
4. Children don't use AI tools for emotional support; the curriculum teaches them when to go to a trusted adult instead.
5. Every AI-generated item a child uses is labelled “made with AI” in the portfolio.

### Teacher enablement

This answers the parent complaint that “tutors aren't trained to handle kids and AI.”

- 2-day hands-on training per tier, where teachers build 3 of the projects themselves.
- A period-by-period facilitator guide with timings, Tanglish scripts, sample answers, and a “what if it goes wrong” box, in the same style as our Cohort 1 guide.
- Ready-to-print Think Books (with a Fail Log), templates and certificates. Teachers who complete a full tier become BrainLIT Certified Think-First Teachers.
- A monthly BrainLIT mentor visit or call, plus a teachers' WhatsApp support group.
- Partner schools receive a printable term schedule and a parent letter for each project.

## Assessment and proof

We assess the thinking, not the polish of the AI output. A child who caught AI's mistake and explained it scores higher than a child with a prettier poster and no reasons.

| What we measure | How | When | Who sees it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Think-First score | The same 15-minute task at the start and end of the year: a problem plus an AI answer that has 3 planted errors. We count the questions asked, errors caught and reasons given. | Weeks 1 and 30 | School leadership, parents |
| 5 Thinking Muscles rubric | A 4-level rubric per muscle (Beginning, Developing, Strong, Leading), scored on every Show step | Every project | Teacher; termly report to parents |
| Project completion | Number of outputs delivered to a real user | Running count | School dashboard |
| Portfolio | One page per project, with the child's part and the AI's part marked | Ongoing; printed at Demo Day | Parents, inspectors, admissions visitors |
| Voice of the child | A 60-second “Before, I \_\_\_. Now I \_\_\_.” video | End of tier | Parents; school social media, with consent |

This directly fills the proof gap in our positioning work: we have student work today, but no pre/post measure. A school pilot gives us both.

**Benefits the school can show:**

- A visible AI programme for admissions season
- Demo Day as a parent-engagement event
- Real fixes on campus (sorted waste, fixed taps, a reception help bot)
- Children trained to use AI safely before they go online alone
